Calculus is often introduced as a collection of formulas for derivatives and integrals. But geometrically, it asks two complementary questions about a curve:

1. **Slope**: How fast is the quantity changing at a specific instant?
2. **Accumulation**: How much total quantity has built up over an interval?

## Slope as a Linear Approximation

When you zoom in closely on any smooth curve $y = f(x)$, it begins to look like a straight line. The derivative $f'(x)$ is simply the slope of that tangent line:

$$
f(x + h) \approx f(x) + f'(x)h
$$

## The Fundamental Unification

The Fundamental Theorem of Calculus shows that accumulation (area under the curve) and slope (rate of change) are exact inverses of each other. If $A(x)$ measures the area under $f(t)$ from $a$ to $x$:

$$
A'(x) = f(x)
$$

Rate of change of accumulated area equals the height of the curve at that point.
